\title{Lineage Is Memory: Parent Inheritance and Feedback Disclosure in
Short-Budget LLM-Guided Optimization}
\author{Shanglin Yang, Zhan Shi, Li Yin}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle
\begin{abstract}
Iterative prompt and program optimizers such as GEPA refine a candidate
by evaluating it on a search set, showing a proposer model a subset of
failing examples, and generating a revision. Recent methods seek to
improve this loop by expanding what the proposer observes in each round,
using full-set error clustering, targeted minibatches, or trace
inspection, or by upgrading the proposer to a larger model. Because
existing evaluations always condition revisions on a surviving parent
from the search lineage, the contribution of parent inheritance has
remained confounded with the breadth of per-round feedback disclosure.
We separate these factors in a factorial study under matched evaluation
budgets across nine prompt and code optimization benchmarks and two
model families.

Under short evaluation budgets where task rules must be inferred from
feedback, greedy parent inheritance accounts for the dominant share of
optimization progress. Accumulating edits along a parent-child lineage
substantially outperforms resampling from the initial seed across both
model families, even when seed-reset proposals are given a clustered
summary of all search-set failures in every round, whereas upgrading the
proposer to a larger model within the same family yields no comparable
gain. Moreover, parent inheritance alters the value of wider per-round
feedback: full-set error clustering and diverse failure selection
improve accuracy when resetting to the seed and accelerate learning in
the opening rounds of search, but sequential parent inheritance rapidly
closes this gap as solved error categories are retained in the candidate
and filtered from subsequent failure lists. On classification tasks
where target labels appear only in evaluator feedback, instructing the
proposer to copy revealed facts into the candidate prompt allows parent
inheritance to accumulate the label inventory across rounds and surpass
GEPA at less than half of its token cost, whereas the same instruction
fails under seed resetting and slightly degrades accuracy when all
options are already given in the seed. Finally, call-type token
accounting shows that GEPA expends the vast majority of its tokens on
full-set validation and minibatch gating, with only a small fraction
spent on proposer reflection.
\end{abstract}

\hypertarget{introduction}{%
\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}}

Reflective prompt and program optimizers refine candidate instructions
or code through an iterative propose-and-evaluate loop. In GEPA
\citep{a2025gepa}, the system runs a candidate instruction on a small
minibatch, presents the inputs, outputs, and evaluator feedback to a
proposer model, accepts the revision if it improves minibatch
performance, and selects subsequent parents from a Pareto front. Similar
evolutionary loops underlie OPRO \citep{yang2023large}, EvoPrompt
\citep{guo2023evoprompt}, Promptbreeder
\citep{fernando2023promptbreeder}, FunSearch
\citep{romeraparedes2023mathematical}, and AlphaEvolve
\citep{alexander2025alphaevolve}.

Recent work has focused on widening what the proposer observes during
reflection. \citet{liu2026espo} note via a coupon-collector calculation
that a random minibatch of three examples exposes only a small fraction
of error modes per round, and propose ESPO, which clusters all training
errors into three to seven diagnostic patterns before generating edits.
RPT \citep{bayat2026reflective} provides a tool that aggregates
critiques across the training set, AdaGEPA \citep{chen2026adagepa}
steers minibatch slots toward identified weaknesses, Contrastive
Reflection \citep{koh2026contrastive} pairs failing and passing examples
from decision-tree slices, and CASD \citep{singh2026coding} lets a
coding agent inspect the full trace corpus. In all of these iterative
studies, revisions are tested only inside lineage-preserving search,
where every proposal edits a surviving parent. Without a lineage-off
control that resets each proposal to the initial seed, existing
evaluations cannot tell how much progress comes from wider single-round
reflection and how much comes from accumulating edits along a
parent-child lineage.

To separate these factors, we organize an optimizer around three design
choices: lineage \(P\), feedback disclosure \(F\), and evaluation
scheduling \(A\). Lineage \(P\) determines whether each proposal edits
the initial seed \(P = \text{seed}\), which corresponds to best-of-\(N\)
resampling from the seed's feedback, or edits the current search-best
candidate \(P = \text{parent}\). Disclosure \(F\) determines whether the
evaluator shows the proposer only the scalar score, the first \(k\)
failures, \(k\) structurally diverse failures, a random mixed minibatch
of \(k\) passing and failing examples, or an LLM-clustered summary of
all failures with or without expected answers. Scheduling \(A\) governs
candidate evaluation and stopping. We run a crossed \(P \times F\)
factorial experiment under a fixed full-search-set scheduler with early
stopping, complemented by a system-level comparison against GEPA under
matched maximum task-model call caps. Across 1,750 runs on nine task
families, comprising five constructed rule-discovery tasks, a
rubric-judged customer support task, ScoNe \citep{she2023scone}, a
12-intent subset of Banking77 \citep{casanueva2020efficient}, and
BIG-Bench Hard salient translation error detection
\citep{suzgun2022challenging}, we replicate the main contrasts on
\texttt{claude-haiku-5-5} and test a larger proposer model
\texttt{gpt-4.1}.

Our main findings are as follows:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Parent inheritance outweighs single-round disclosure breadth
  and proposer size (Sections 4.1--4.2):} On tasks where rules must be
  recovered from feedback, switching from \(P = \text{seed}\) to
  \(P = \text{parent}\) produces the largest accuracy gain in our study,
  adding +0.238 across all seven primary tasks on \texttt{gpt-4.1-mini}
  and +0.222 across five tasks on \texttt{claude-haiku-5-5}. This
  advantage holds even when seed-reset proposals draw fresh random
  examples each round in \texttt{seed-minibatch} or receive a clustered
  summary of all search-set failures in every round in
  \texttt{seed-clustergold}: showing just four raw failures under parent
  inheritance still beats \texttt{seed-clustergold} by +0.157. Upgrading
  the proposer from \texttt{gpt-4.1-mini} to \texttt{gpt-4.1} on three
  feedback-dependent prompt tasks yields no clear gain in either row,
  while parent inheritance under \texttt{gpt-4.1} adds +0.380.
\item
  \textbf{Fact retention in the prompt explains the Banking77 gap with
  GEPA (Section 4.3):} On the 12-intent Banking77 task, where intent
  names appear only in error feedback, baseline \texttt{parent-first}
  initially scored 0.372 against 0.664 for GEPA. Inspection of the
  search logs showed that early formatting-only edits scored 0.000 and
  stayed anchored to the seed, whereas GEPA's reflection prompt
  explicitly tells the proposer to copy domain facts from feedback into
  the instruction. In a follow-up experiment on Banking77 and BBH-STE,
  appending one sentence with this instruction raised
  \texttt{parent-first} on Banking77 from 0.372 to 0.856 at 43\% of
  GEPA's median token cost, while \texttt{seed-first} with the same
  sentence retained only the 4 intents shown in round 1 and scored
  0.317. On BBH-STE, where all six option labels are already in the
  seed, the same sentence reduced accuracy by −0.061.
\item
  \textbf{Parent inheritance closes the gap with wider per-round
  disclosure over multiple rounds (Sections 4.4--4.6):} When resetting
  to the seed, showing only the first four failures crowds out
  zero-feedback code priors on unshown error categories, and both
  diverse sampling and target-preserving clustering improve accuracy
  over \texttt{first-k} disclosure. Under parent inheritance, full-set
  clustering initially leads in round 2 by +0.173, but as solved
  categories are retained in the parent and filtered from future failure
  lists, \texttt{first-k} disclosure shifts across 46\% to 86\% of the
  search set, catching up by round 4 and finishing at the same level.
  Conversely, random mixed minibatches reduce parent-inheritance
  accuracy by spending feedback slots on passing examples.
\item
  \textbf{GEPA spends most of its token budget on validation and
  minibatch gating (Section 4.7):} Across all seven prompt tasks
  evaluated with GEPA, proposer reflection consumes only 2\% to 15\% of
  total tokens, while full search-set validations and minibatch checks
  consume the remaining 85\% to 98\%.
\end{itemize}

\hypertarget{related-work}{%
\section{Related Work}\label{related-work}}

\hypertarget{llm-guided-program-evolution}{%
\subsection{LLM-Guided Program
Evolution}\label{llm-guided-program-evolution}}

Evolutionary search with language models, as in FunSearch
\citep{romeraparedes2023mathematical}, AlphaEvolve
\citep{alexander2025alphaevolve}, and ImprovEvolve
\citep{kravatskiy2026improvevolve}, maintains an archive of high-scoring
programs and prompts a model to mutate or recombine selected parents.
Performance gains in this literature are typically credited to the
evolutionary loop as a whole. The closest methodological study is
Evolution or Illusion? \citep{oved2026evolution}, which varies how a
fixed generation budget is split between initial seeds and evolutionary
generations, showing that strategy rankings can reverse across budget
scales. \citet{oved2026evolution} hold feedback presentation fixed, do
not break down token usage by call type, and treat best-of-\(N\)
resampling only as a reference curve. We complement their work by fixing
the evaluation budget cap and crossing parent inheritance with feedback
disclosure.

\hypertarget{reflective-prompt-optimization}{%
\subsection{Reflective Prompt
Optimization}\label{reflective-prompt-optimization}}

Early automatic prompt optimizers either backpropagated textual
critiques from failing examples, as in ProTeGi
\citep{pryzant2023automatic} and TextGrad
\citep{yuksekgonul2024textgrad}, or showed the proposer a score-ordered
history of past instructions, as in OPRO \citep{yang2023large} and
EvoPrompt \citep{guo2023evoprompt}. GEPA \citep{a2025gepa} combined
reflection on a small random minibatch of default size \(b=3\) with
Pareto-front parent selection and minibatch acceptance gating. GEPA
0.0.7 also includes a prompt instruction asking the reflection model to
copy domain-specific facts from feedback into the revised prompt;
Section 4.3 tests this instruction directly. Other recent studies
examine when reflective optimization helps or fails:
\citet{lumer2026reflective} show that per-dimension feedback transfers
better than scalar scores under noisy judges;
\citet{giummarra2026understanding} find that GEPA improves MATH-500
accuracy but fails to improve on HotpotQA with local reasoning models
while increasing token cost; and \citet{ma2024large} show that prompt
optimizers rely heavily on the proposer's pre-trained task prior,
matching our results on \texttt{dates} and \texttt{scone}, where the
model prior already fits the specification.

\hypertarget{expanding-reflection-scope}{%
\subsection{Expanding Reflection
Scope}\label{expanding-reflection-scope}}

Several recent papers argue that small random minibatches under-sample
the error distribution during reflection. \citet{liu2026espo} formalize
this via a coupon-collector bound and introduce ESPO, which clusters all
training failures into three to seven error patterns in a Diagnose stage
before generating pattern-specific edits in a Diversify stage and
bootstrap-selecting among them in a Stabilize stage. RPT
\citep{bayat2026reflective} gives the optimizer a clustering tool over
full-set critiques; AdaGEPA \citep{chen2026adagepa} swaps minibatch
slots toward identified failure modes; CASD \citep{singh2026coding}
replaces iterative search with a coding agent that reads all traces
before writing a single prompt; and Contrastive Reflection
\citep{koh2026contrastive} pairs failing and passing examples from
decision-tree slices. Every iterative method in this group is evaluated
only inside a lineage-preserving loop, without a seed-reset control or a
zero-feedback baseline. Testing disclosure schemes with and without
parent inheritance shows that single-round error coverage strongly
limits seed-reset optimization, whereas under parent inheritance its
terminal effect is much smaller.

\hypertarget{validation-efficiency-and-example-selection}{%
\subsection{Validation Efficiency and Example
Selection}\label{validation-efficiency-and-example-selection}}

Explore More, Validate Less \citep{zhou2026explore} notes that GEPA
wastes evaluations on minibatches where the parent already scores
perfectly, producing no edit, and proposes trimming full-set
validations. Section 4.7 extends this by decomposing GEPA's token budget
across reflection, minibatch gating, and full validation. Choosing a
small representative set of examples to specify program behavior is also
a classic problem in inductive program synthesis
\citep{pu2017selecting}; our \texttt{diverse} condition adapts
farthest-point input selection to iterative error feedback.

\hypertarget{methodology-and-experimental-design}{%
\section{Methodology and Experimental
Design}\label{methodology-and-experimental-design}}

\hypertarget{the-pfa-optimization-loop}{%
\subsection{The P/F/A Optimization
Loop}\label{the-pfa-optimization-loop}}

We study iterative optimization over a candidate space \(\mathcal{C}\)
consisting of natural language system prompts or Python functions. Each
task defines a search set
\(\mathcal{D}_{\text{search}} = \{(x_i, y^*_i)\}_{i=1}^{N_{\text{search}}}\)
partitioned into \(G\) failure categories
\(\{C^{\text{search}}_1, \dots, C^{\text{search}}_G\}\), and a disjoint
held-out test set
\(\mathcal{D}_{\text{holdout}} = \{(x'_j, y'^*_j)\}_{j=1}^{N_{\text{holdout}}}\)
partitioned into
\(\{C^{\text{holdout}}_1, \dots, C^{\text{holdout}}_G\}\) that is never
accessed during search. An evaluator \(E(c, \mathcal{D})\) runs
candidate \(c \in \mathcal{C}\) on \(\mathcal{D}\), returning binary
instance scores \(s_i(c) \in \{0, 1\}\), accuracy
\(S(c, \mathcal{D}) = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}|}\sum_{i} s_i(c)\), and
failing indices \(\mathcal{E}(c) = \{i : s_i(c) = 0\}\).

\textbf{Algorithm 1: Controlled P/F/A Optimization Loop}

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Input:} Seed candidate \(c_0 \in \mathcal{C}\), evaluation
  budget cap \(B\), lineage policy
  \(P \in \{\text{seed}, \text{parent}\}\), disclosure operator \(F\),
  search set \(\mathcal{D}_{\text{search}}\), held-out set
  \(\mathcal{D}_{\text{holdout}}\).
\item
  \textbf{Initialize:} Evaluate \(c_0\) on
  \(\mathcal{D}_{\text{search}}\) and cache
  \(E(c_0, \mathcal{D}_{\text{search}}) = \big(S(c_0, \mathcal{D}_{\text{search}}), \mathcal{E}(c_0)\big)\).
  Set history \(\mathcal{H}_0 = \{c_0\}\) and current best
  \(c^*_0 = c_0\).
\item
  \textbf{For} round \(t = 1, \dots, B - 1\) \textbf{do:}

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    \textbf{Stop check (\(A\)):} If
    \(S(c^*_{t-1}, \mathcal{D}_{\text{search}}) = 1.0\), stop early.
  \item
    \textbf{Parent selection (\(P\)):} Set \(p_t = c_0\) if
    \(P = \text{seed}\), or \(p_t = c^*_{t-1}\) if
    \(P = \text{parent}\). Retrieve cached
    \(E(p_t, \mathcal{D}_{\text{search}})\).
  \item
    \textbf{Feedback disclosure (\(F\)):} Build feedback string
    \(f_t = F(p_t, \mathcal{D}_{\text{search}}, k=4)\) from
    \(E(p_t, \mathcal{D}_{\text{search}})\).
  \item
    \textbf{Proposal:} Query proposer \(M_{\text{prop}}\) with
    \texttt{PROPOSE\_TEMPLATE} (Appendix B.1) using the task objective,
    \(p_t\), and \(f_t\) to generate \(c_t\).
  \item
    \textbf{Evaluation (\(A\)):} Evaluate \(c_t\) on
    \(\mathcal{D}_{\text{search}}\), consuming 1 evaluation from cap
    \(B\), and cache \(E(c_t, \mathcal{D}_{\text{search}})\). If
    \(S(c_t, \mathcal{D}_{\text{search}}) > S(c^*_{t-1}, \mathcal{D}_{\text{search}})\),
    set \(c^*_t = c_t\); otherwise keep \(c^*_t = c^*_{t-1}\) under
    strict improvement tie-breaking.
  \end{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{Output:} Evaluate \(c^*\) once on
  \(\mathcal{D}_{\text{holdout}}\) and return held-out accuracy
  \(Y = S(c^*, \mathcal{D}_{\text{holdout}})\).
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Factor 1: Lineage (\(P\)).} Under \(P = \text{seed}\), every
round sets \(p_t = c_0\) and reuses the cached evaluation of \(c_0\).
Under deterministic disclosure schemes such as \texttt{none},
\texttt{first}, and \texttt{diverse}, both \(p_t\) and \(f_t\) stay
fixed across rounds, so the \(B-1\) proposals are independent samples
from \(M_{\text{prop}}\) at default provider temperature conditioned on
\(c_0\). Under \texttt{minibatch}, \texttt{cluster}, and
\texttt{clustergold}, \(f_t\) varies across rounds while still requiring
a single-edit jump from \(c_0\). Under \(P = \text{parent}\), every
round edits the current search-best candidate \(p_t = c^*_{t-1}\),
reusing its cached evaluation if the previous proposal did not strictly
improve on it. When \(S(c_0, \mathcal{D}_{\text{search}}) = 0.0\), as on
\texttt{labels}, \texttt{routing}, \texttt{replies}, and
\texttt{banking77}, strict-improvement tie-breaking keeps \(p_t = c_0\)
until a proposal scores above zero; this occurs in round 1 on
\texttt{routing} and \texttt{replies} and in round 1 or 2 on
\texttt{labels}, but can take several rounds on \texttt{banking77}
(Section 4.3). Once \(c^*_t\) improves over \(c_0\),
\(P = \text{parent}\) combines three mechanisms relative to
\(P = \text{seed}\): text inheritance, which keeps discovered rules in
\(p_{t+1}\); residual-error exposure shift, which removes solved cases
from \(\mathcal{E}(p_{t+1})\) so new categories enter the \(k=4\)
display window; and multi-step edit decomposition, which allows the
proposer to fix a subset of categories per round. Secondary inspiration
candidates are disabled in all cells.

\textbf{Factor 2: Feedback Disclosure (\(F\)).} Failing examples are
formatted with their input, wrong output, and expected answer or grading
rubric (Appendix B). We compare six operators: - \texttt{none}: Masks
the evaluation of \(p_t\) by setting \texttt{score=None}, omitting
instance scores, and rendering \texttt{\{\{\ feedback\ \}\}} as
\texttt{(no\ feedback)}. Because \(p_t = c_0\) carries no
round-dependent signal under \texttt{none}, \texttt{seed-none} is the
shared zero-feedback baseline. - \texttt{first}: Shows
\(S(p_t, \mathcal{D}_{\text{search}})\), per-instance binary scores, and
the first \(k = 4\) failures of \(p_t\) in dataset order, followed by
the count of remaining failures. In round 1, this exposes 2 categories
on \texttt{labels}, \texttt{dates}, \texttt{replies}, and \texttt{size},
up to 3 on \texttt{duration}, and up to 4 on \texttt{routing},
\texttt{banking77}, and \texttt{bbh\_ste}. - \texttt{diverse}: Selects
\(k = 4\) failures via greedy farthest-point sampling under normalized
Levenshtein distance over a structural input signature defined by
\texttt{\_shape} in \texttt{presets.py}, which maps lowercase letters to
\texttt{a}, uppercase letters to \texttt{A}, and digits to \texttt{9}
while preserving punctuation, starting from the first failure. -
\texttt{minibatch}: Draws \(k = 4\) examples uniformly at random without
replacement from \(\mathcal{D}_{\text{search}}\) in each round using
\texttt{random.Random(1000\ +\ r)} for run seed
\(r \in \{0, \dots, 9\}\), and shows whether \(p_t\) passed or failed
each drawn example alongside its expected answer, while still gating
acceptance on the full search set. - \texttt{cluster}: Passes all
failures \(\mathcal{E}(p_t)\) to one call of the clusterer model, which
matches \(M_{\text{prop}}\) and groups them into 3 to 7 patterns,
outputting one line per pattern with a description, failure count, and
one verbatim representative wrong answer (\texttt{CLUSTER\_PROMPT},
Appendix B.3). Clustering tokens are included in search tokens. -
\texttt{clustergold}: Identical to \texttt{cluster}, except the prompt
requires each representative failure to include the input, wrong answer,
and full expected answer (\texttt{CLUSTER\_GOLD\_PROMPT}, Appendix B.3).

\textbf{Scheduling (\(A\)) and GEPA Comparison.} In our \(P \times F\)
grid, every proposal is evaluated on all of
\(\mathcal{D}_{\text{search}}\) up to \(B = 8\) evaluations for prompt
tasks, corresponding to \(c_0\) plus 7 proposals and at most
\(8 \times N_{\text{search}}\) task calls, and \(B = 12\) evaluations
for code tasks, corresponding to \(c_0\) plus 11 proposals executed with
a 60-second subprocess timeout across 24 test cases for at most 288
executions. Missing fenced blocks trigger at most one retry, though all
1,750 runs produced valid blocks on the first try; code exceptions score
\(0.0\) with the traceback included in feedback. Proposer, task, and
clusterer models use default provider sampling in \texttt{openai} SDK
2.44.0 for \texttt{gpt-4.1-mini} and \texttt{gpt-4.1} and
\texttt{anthropic} SDK 0.112.0 with default adaptive thinking for
\texttt{claude-haiku-5-5}, while the \texttt{replies} judge uses
\texttt{gpt-4.1-mini} at \texttt{temperature=0} with response caching.
For system-level comparison, we run \texttt{gepa} 0.0.7
\citep{a2025gepa} with default minibatch size \(b = 3\), Pareto
selection, minibatch acceptance gating, its built-in reflection prompt,
and the same task-call cap
\(\text{max\_metric\_calls} = 8 \times N_{\text{search}}\), testing both
unmodified \texttt{GEPA\ mb3} and early-stopped
\texttt{GEPA\ mb3\ +\ stop}.

\hypertarget{statistical-estimands-bootstrap-protocol-and-run-accounting}{%
\subsection{Statistical Estimands, Bootstrap Protocol, and Run
Accounting}\label{statistical-estimands-bootstrap-protocol-and-run-accounting}}

The replication unit is an optimization run with seed
\(r \in \{0, \dots, 9\}\), giving \(n = 10\) runs per cell. Let
\(Y_{\tau,P,F,r} \in [0, 1]\) denote held-out accuracy
\(S(c^*, \mathcal{D}_{\text{holdout}})\) on task
\(\tau \in \mathcal{T}\), reserving \(t\) for optimization rounds, and
let \(\bar{Y}_{\tau,P,F} = \frac{1}{10}\sum_{r=0}^9 Y_{\tau,P,F,r}\)
denote the cell mean. Weighting tasks in \(\mathcal{T}\) equally, we
estimate three quantities:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Lineage main effect (\(\Delta_{\text{lineage}}\)):}
  \[\Delta_{\text{lineage}} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}| \cdot |\mathcal{F}|} \sum_{\tau \in \mathcal{T}} \sum_{F \in \mathcal{F}} \left(\bar{Y}_{\tau,\text{parent},F} - \bar{Y}_{\tau,\text{seed},F}\right).\]
\item
  \textbf{Attenuation interaction (\(I_X\)):} For
  \(X \in \{\text{diverse}, \text{minibatch}, \text{cluster}, \text{clustergold}\}\)
  and
  \(\delta_{\tau,P}(X) = \bar{Y}_{\tau,P,X} - \bar{Y}_{\tau,P,\text{first}}\),
  \[I_X = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}|} \sum_{\tau \in \mathcal{T}} \big(\delta_{\tau,\text{seed}}(X) - \delta_{\tau,\text{parent}}(X)\big).\]
  A positive \(I_X > 0\) means disclosure \(X\) improves over
  \texttt{first} more under seed resetting than under parent
  inheritance.
\item
  \textbf{Unshown-category proposal coverage
  (\(\text{Cov}_{\text{unshown}}\)):} In the \texttt{seed} row,
  \(\mathcal{U}_\tau \subset \{1, \dots, G\}\) is the set of categories
  where \(c_0\) fails at least one search example in \(>50\%\) of
  \texttt{seed} runs, excluding categories shown among the first four
  failures in \(>50\%\) of \texttt{seed-first} runs, with
  \texttt{seed-none} using the same reference set \(\mathcal{U}_\tau\).
  Across proposals \(\mathcal{V}_r = \{c_1, \dots, c_{m_r}\}\) in run
  \(r\),
  \[\text{Cov}_{\text{unshown}}(r) = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{U}_\tau|} \sum_{g \in \mathcal{U}_\tau} \frac{1}{|\mathcal{V}_r|} \sum_{c \in \mathcal{V}_r} \mathbf{1}\!\left[s_i(c) = 1 \text{ for all } i \in C^{\text{eval}}_g\right],\]
  where \(C^{\text{eval}}_g\) is \(C^{\text{search}}_g\) for prompt
  tasks and \(C^{\text{holdout}}_g\), evaluated offline after search
  completes, for code tasks. Crowding-out (\(\mathcal{H}_{C1}\)) holds
  when
  \(\bar{\text{Cov}}_{\text{unshown}}(\text{seed-first}) - \bar{\text{Cov}}_{\text{unshown}}(\text{seed-none}) < 0\).
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Bootstrap inference and run accounting.} All confidence
intervals are 95\% percentile bootstrap intervals given by the 1-indexed
order statistics \([\tilde{\psi}_{(251)}, \tilde{\psi}_{(9750)}]\) over
\(N_{\text{boot}} = 10{,}000\) task-stratified resamples initialized
with \texttt{random.Random(0)} in \texttt{results/analyze.py},
resampling 10 runs with replacement within each task-by-condition cell.
For conditional tokens-to-solve ratios (\(\mathcal{H}_{C3}\)), solved
runs are resampled within each arm on tasks with at least \(3/10\)
solved runs per arm. Alongside these fixed-strata intervals, we report
per-task intervals (Table 2A), leave-one-task-out intervals (Table 8),
and two-stage task-cluster bootstrap intervals that resample tasks at
the outer level: on \texttt{gpt-4.1-mini}, the 7-task cluster interval
is {[}+0.104, +0.363{]} for \(\Delta_{\text{lineage}}\) and {[}+0.052,
+0.336{]} for \(I_{\text{clustergold}}\), which is positive on all 7
tasks with exact two-sided sign test \(p = 0.0156\). All 1,750 completed
runs in \texttt{results/runs.jsonl} come from five sequential
pre-registered batches documented in Table 7 and Appendix A: Batch A.1
with 750 runs, Batch A.2 with 140 runs, Batch A.3 with 350 runs, Batch
A.4 with 470 runs, and Batch A.5 with 40 runs.

increasing 7-task held-out accuracy by

To test whether explicit fact copying accounts for this gap, we
registered a follow-up batch (Appendix A.5) that appended one sentence,
\texttt{CARRY\_FACTS} (Appendix B.1), to the proposer objective:
\emph{``The task model sees only this prompt, never the feedback: copy
every task-specific fact the feedback reveals (for example the exact
expected labels or formats) into the prompt.''} On \texttt{banking77}
(Table 4, Panel B and Figure 2), appending \texttt{CARRY\_FACTS} raised
\texttt{parent-first} accuracy from 0.372 to 0.856, with all 10 runs
improving in round 1 and surpassing \texttt{GEPA\ mb3} by +0.192 at 43\%
of its median token cost, using 45.8k versus 106.9k tokens. This gain
requires parent inheritance: \texttt{seed-first\ +\ carry} observes the
same 4 seed failures every round, writes 4 intents into the prompt in
every run, and scores 0.317, whereas \texttt{parent-first\ +\ carry}
accumulates 11 to 12 intents across rounds with a mean of 11.7 out of
12. Conversely, on \texttt{bbh\_ste}, where all six option labels are
already given in the seed, appending \texttt{CARRY\_FACTS} reduced
\texttt{parent-first} accuracy from 0.653 to 0.592, a change of −0.061
that fails non-inferiority test \(\mathcal{H}_{F3}\). Copying feedback
facts into the prompt helps when label inventories must be discovered
from feedback, and slightly hurts when the specification is already
complete in the seed.

\hypertarget{discussion}{%
\section{Discussion}\label{discussion}}

\textbf{Why parent inheritance substitutes for wide single-round
disclosure.} Under seed resetting, the proposer has no persistent state,
so a narrow feedback sample can anchor the rewrite on displayed formats
and crowd out zero-feedback priors on unshown categories (Table 5, Panel
A). Even when a full-set summary in \texttt{seed-clustergold} exposes
all failing categories, synthesizing every rule from scratch in a single
edit reaches only 0.723 across the five feedback-dependent tasks. Under
parent inheritance, the candidate text retains rules discovered in
earlier rounds, preserving 70\% to 95\% of solved cases, while full
search-set evaluation removes those solved cases from future failure
lists, shifting \texttt{first-k} disclosure across 46\% to 86\% of
\(\mathcal{D}_{\text{search}}\) over a run (Table 5, Panel B).
Consequently, while \texttt{clustergold} speeds up learning in rounds 1
and 2, \texttt{parent-first} catches up by round 4 (Table 5C). On tasks
such as \texttt{banking77} where target labels are hidden in feedback,
this accumulation works only if the proposer writes revealed facts into
the candidate prompt via \texttt{CARRY\_FACTS}; when all options are
already in the seed, as on \texttt{bbh\_ste}, copying training facts
slightly hurts generalization.

\textbf{Implications for evaluating reflective optimizers.} Because
recent reflection-scope methods such as ESPO \citep{liu2026espo}, RPT
\citep{bayat2026reflective}, and AdaGEPA \citep{chen2026adagepa} are
tested only inside lineage-preserving loops, their reported gains
measure residual improvements on top of parent inheritance. In our grid,
\texttt{parent-clustergold} beats \texttt{parent-minibatch} by +0.043 on
\texttt{gpt-4.1-mini} and +0.086 on Haiku, but does not beat
\texttt{parent-first} at termination. Gains over default GEPA in
short-budget settings can therefore come from replacing mixed pass/fail
minibatches with pure failure feedback rather than from clustering
itself. Future evaluations of reflection-scope or proposer-upgrade
methods should include three controls: a lineage-off \texttt{seed}
baseline row alongside the \texttt{parent} row, a zero-feedback
\texttt{seed-none} baseline to detect crowding-out, and call-type token
accounting separating reflection from validation.

\textbf{Practical guidelines.} When tuning prompts or code under short
evaluation budgets, practitioners should build on the best parent
candidate while monitoring for zero-score stalls on tasks with hidden
labels, show raw failing examples with full expected answers instead of
random pass/fail minibatches, instruct the proposer to copy revealed
task rules or labels into the prompt when they are absent from the seed,
and reserve full-set error clustering for one-shot repair, parallel
best-of-\(N\) sampling, or the first two rounds of search.

\hypertarget{limitations}{%
\section{Limitations}\label{limitations}}

Several constraints bound our conclusions. First, five of the seven
primary tasks and \texttt{replies} are constructed by withholding rules
from the seed, which favors cross-round accumulation; on the two public
extension tasks, \texttt{banking77} and \texttt{bbh\_ste}, the pooled
lineage effect is smaller at +0.088 versus +0.225 across the same four
disclosure schemes on the primary tasks, and the disclosure interactions
\(\mathcal{H}_{P2}\text{--}\mathcal{H}_{P5}\) are not statistically
resolved. Grouping search examples by category on constructed tasks also
reduces round-1 category diversity under \texttt{first}, and on larger
tasks where \(B\) rounds of \(k=4\) failures cannot cover the error
space, wider per-round disclosure may retain an advantage beyond round
4. Second, while \texttt{parent-clustergold} versus
\texttt{seed-clustergold} shows that parent inheritance adds +0.139
overall and +0.198 on non-ceiling tasks even when all failure categories
are summarized every round, \texttt{seed-clustergold} compresses
failures into 3 to 7 patterns; fully separating text inheritance from
residual-error exposure under raw feedback would require controls that
reset to \(c_0\) while showing all raw failures or the cumulative union
of parent-trajectory failures. Third, our comparison with GEPA 0.0.7 is
system-level across parent selection, minibatch gating, minibatch size,
and reflection wording. Fourth, \texttt{carry-facts} was designed after
inspecting Batch A.4 \texttt{banking77} logs and tested on two public
benchmarks, helping \texttt{banking77} by +0.483 and hurting
\texttt{bbh\_ste} by −0.061. Fifth, \texttt{banking77} was subsampled to
12 intents and \texttt{bbh\_ste} to 198 examples, with held-out sets of
12 to 72 examples across tasks, to fit the 8-evaluation budget and
300-character feedback window. Sixth, \texttt{dates} and \texttt{scone}
start near ceiling. Seventh, \texttt{cluster} and \texttt{clustergold}
test only the Diagnose stage of ESPO \citep{liu2026espo}, and
\texttt{diverse} uses surface Levenshtein distance. Eighth, experiments
use two model families, \texttt{gpt-4.1-mini} / \texttt{gpt-4.1} and
\texttt{claude-haiku-5-5} with default adaptive thinking, and a single
\texttt{gpt-4.1-mini} judge on \texttt{replies}. Finally, batches ran
sequentially over two days, and three intermediate batches interrupted
by API quota exhaustion totaling 267 aborted run attempts were
quarantined and re-run from scratch under pre-registered rules (Appendix
A).
